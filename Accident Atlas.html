Probability is the volume under the map

A made-up town where accidents follow one joint density, fXY(x, y) = (3/2)x on the wedge 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, −x ≤ y ≤ x. Colour shows how crowded accidents are. The volume under it is probability. Everything below, from marginals to the CDF, is built on this one map.

No y in the formula means flat north–south

Hold x fixed and walk north or south: the density stays at (3/2)x the whole way. Every stretch of the same length on that street is equally risky. A missing variable means uniform in that direction, never absent.

Outside the wedge, nothing happens

Where |y| > x the density is 0. No height means no volume, so no probability. The wedge's edges are where y still matters: they tie the north–south range to x.

Height is density; volume is probability

To get a probability, integrate over an area: P((X, Y) ∈ A) = ∬A fXY dx dy. The whole wedge holds ∫01 (3/2)x · 2x dx = 1.

Squash the map to get a marginal

The marginal of X answers one question: ignoring north and south, how are accidents spread from west to east? At each x, add up the density along the whole north–south strip.

fX(x) = ∫−xx (3/2)x dy = 3x2
fY(y) = ∫|y|1 (3/2)x dx = (3/4)(1 − y2)

That is not the side view. At y = 0 the surface only rises like (3/2)x, but further east each strip is also wider, so more volume piles up.

Each dot is one simulated accident. Pick a direction to squash them.

Slice one street to get a conditional

Fix a north–south street x = x0. Along it, the density is flat at (3/2)x0 from y = −x0 to x0. That slice has area 3x02, which is exactly fX(x0). Divide by it and the area becomes 1:

fY|X(y | x0) = fXY / fX = 1/(2x0), for −x0 ≤ y ≤ x0

Once you know the street, every point along it is equally likely. Short streets in the west get tall, narrow conditionals; long streets in the east get low, wide ones.

So X and Y are dependent

Take the point (0.5, 0.8). It sits outside the wedge, so fXY = 0. But fX(0.5) · fY(0.8) = 0.75 × 0.27 = 0.2025. Independence needs fXY = fXfY everywhere, so it fails. Learning that y = 0.8 tells you x ≥ 0.8.

A single street has zero probability

In a continuous model, probability needs width. The chance that an accident lands within w/2 of the street x = 0.5 is

P = (0.5 + w/2)3 − (0.5 − w/2)3 = 0.75w + w3/4

Shrink the width to zero and the probability goes to zero, so P(X = 0.5) = 0. The leading term 0.75w is fX(0.5) × w: density is probability per unit of width.

If you only record which street pair (3rd Ave and 5th St), switch to a joint pmf p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y), a table with one cell per intersection. Then each street does carry its own probability.

One road: find k so the area is 1

Now take a single road. Accidents along it follow f(x) = k(3 − x2) for −1 ≤ x ≤ 1, and 0 elsewhere. Every density encloses total area 1, and that pins down k.

∫−11 k(3 − x2) dx = k[3x − x3/3]−11 = k(8/3 + 8/3) = (16/3)k
(16/3)k = 1  ⇒  k = 3/16

The road is busiest at the centre, f(0) = 9/16 ≈ 0.56, and quietest at the ends, f(±1) = 3/8. It never drops to zero inside the road.

F(x) adds up the area as you move along the road

F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is the area under f from the west end up to x. Use t inside the integral so it doesn't clash with the limit.

F(x) = ∫−1x (3/16)(3 − t2) dt = (8 + 9x − x3)/16 for −1 ≤ x ≤ 1
F(x) = 0 for x < −1,   F(x) = 1 for x > 1

Checks: F(−1) = 0, F(0) = 1/2 because f is symmetric, F(1) = 1, and F′(x) = f(x). Probabilities become subtraction, P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a): the shaded area on top equals the jump on the bottom.

Map: an accident happened on the street x = 0.25. What is the chance it was north of y = 0.1?

Given x = 0.25, Y is uniform on [−0.25, 0.25] at height 1/(2 × 0.25) = 2, so P = 2 × (0.25 − 0.1) = 0.3
